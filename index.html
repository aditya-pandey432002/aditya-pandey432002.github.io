Fixing ticket booking for high-demand events on BookMyShow

NextLeap Product Manager Fellowship capstone, 2024. Awarded Top Fellow.

During sales for events like Coldplay and the Cricket World Cup, fans hit crashes, surprise logouts and long queues, then abandoned checkout, just as Zomato District was competing for the same events.

The lever
I broke revenue down to conversion from event page to cart, checkout and payment, the one number a product change could move without adding server load.
What users said
21 users surveyed. They wanted to see their live queue position, and 62% were first-time concert-goers confused by seating tiers.
What I proposed
A VIP tiered queue for loyal buyers, with a lottery that gives everyone else a fair second chance at tickets.
Read the full approachHide the full approach

Market context

BookMyShow's operating revenue grew from ₹277 Cr in FY22 to ₹1,400 Cr in FY24, with live events at about a quarter of revenue. Growth was slowing, competition sharpened after Zomato acquired Paytm Insider in August 2024, and customers switch platforms easily.

Research

A survey of 21 users and follow-up interviews showed:

  • Most respondents named missing out on tickets for a high-demand event as a major frustration, and 71% were aged 18 to 34.
  • 62% were first-time concert-goers who didn't understand seating and price tiers, which slowed their decisions at the worst possible moment.
  • Users wanted real-time queue position and ticket availability to trust the process.
  • Because concerts are social, many wanted to book together as a group.

Two personas anchored the design: Ankit, a 25-year-old IT professional who books concerts and cricket often, and Riya, a college student who was logged out mid-booking for Coldplay and lost her place.

Options and how I chose

I grouped four solutions under customer experience and operational efficiency, listed the main risk of each, and scored them. The VIP tiered queue won because it rewards repeat buyers, keeps demand controllable, and uses the lottery to protect fairness for everyone else. Its main risks were lottery fatigue and queue complexity.

Design

Wireframes cover the live events home screen, an event page that offers lottery entry or VIP access, a queue screen with live position and estimated wait, and checkout. The backend routes requests through an API gateway (authentication, rate limiting) to event and booking services, then through VIP eligibility logic into queue management and a separate lottery service.

How I'd measure it

  • North star: ticket sales conversion for VIP users, as a share of available tickets.
  • Supporting: VIP activation rate, time to checkout for VIP users, VIP retention across events, and how many users re-enter a lottery after losing one.

Risks and mitigations

  • Occasional buyers may feel the system is unfair, so a fixed share of tickets is reserved for the lottery and non-VIP users get offers on future events.
  • Expensive tickets can cause payment drop-off, so the design adds a book-now, pay-later option.

Neighbourhood car and bike sharing for Bengaluru

Product concept from primary research, 2025

Bengaluru residents buy cars because public transport is unreliable, then pay EMIs and fuel for vehicles that sit idle most of the week. I tested whether neighbours would share them.

The insight
Interest wasn't the problem; trust was. People would share, but only inside their own community, with ID checks and insurance.
What I proposed
A peer-to-peer platform for gated communities that covers both rentals and carpooling, including two-wheelers.
North star
Successful trips completed, supported by active listings, vehicle utilisation, match conversion and retention.
Read the full approachHide the full approach

The problem

Bengaluru has about 12.4 million registered vehicles, including roughly 2.5 million cars and 8.2 million two-wheelers. A 12 km commute can take hours, metro expansion lags demand, and the city is one of India's largest fuel consumers. Ownership is a financial burden people accept because they want control over their travel.

Who it's for

  • Stretched tech professionals (25 to 35) with high EMIs who want to earn from an idle car.
  • Students and temporary residents (18 to 25) who need flexible transport without buying a vehicle.
  • Families in gated communities who often need a second car for a few hours.

What the research showed

89% of non-owners were open to renting from a neighbour and 80% of owners were open to renting out an idle vehicle. 71% wanted insurance on every trip. Owners' top fears were damage and misuse, each cited by half. One 42-year-old resident said they would never hand a car to a stranger from an app, but would consider someone from their own complex who was verified by the residents' welfare association.

MVP

  • Verification with Aadhaar or PAN, driving licence and community ID.
  • Listings for cars and two-wheelers with owner-set availability and pricing.
  • Search, filters and in-app payment for renters.
  • Carpool matching for recurring commutes, such as to IT parks, with live tracking.
  • In-app chat, mandatory ratings after every trip, and basic insurance on every transaction.

Key flows

I mapped three journeys end to end: a renter discovering, booking, collecting and returning a vehicle; a rider finding and joining a carpool; and an owner listing a vehicle, passing verification, managing requests and receiving payouts.

Go-to-market

Launch community by community: partner with residents' welfare associations for endorsement and access, set up on-ground kiosks for onboarding, give owners listing bonuses and renters first-trip discounts and referral rewards, and run geo-targeted ads inside community boundaries. Feedback from the app and regular association check-ins feeds the next iteration.

Growing an organic skincare brand to $6M a month without buying traffic

APM assignment for Virgio

The brief: grow monthly revenue from $3.75M to $6M in four months with limited acquisition spend and a $500K quarterly budget. With 2.5M monthly users already visiting, the answer had to come from the ones already there.

The diagnosis
Only 2.5% of visitors bought, order value sat flat at $50 in a high-margin category, and 7 in 10 customers never came back.
What I proposed
Nine initiatives across conversion, order value and retention, plus Refill Club, a refill subscription in reusable packaging.
The trade-off
$450K allocated across seven initiatives with $50K held back. AI personalisation and influencer campaigns were deferred.
Read the full approachHide the full approach

Three levers and what moves them

  • Conversion, 2.5% to 4%: verified reviews with customer photos, "shop by skin type" filters, and a skin quiz that leads to a personalised routine page.
  • Order value, $50 to $60: curated AM/PM and concern-based bundles, free shipping above $60 with a clear "add a little more" nudge, and "complete your routine" cross-sells.
  • Retention, 30% to 50%: a points-based loyalty programme with tiers, restock reminders timed to product usage at 30, 60 and 90 days, and post-purchase guides on using products well.

The creative bet: Refill Club

Customers buy a starter kit with a reusable bottle, then receive refill pouches every 30, 60 or 90 days at 10 to 15% less, with free shipping and loyalty points. Skincare is a habit, people forget to restock, and organic buyers care about packaging waste, so the subscription fits how the category is already used. The reusable bottle adds a commitment that makes switching brands feel wasteful.

Rollout

  1. Month 1: pick three hero products with high reorder potential, design the bottle, and sign pouch suppliers.
  2. Month 2: launch a "subscribe and save 15% with free shipping" opt-in at checkout, with email and WhatsApp reminders.
  3. Month 3: track refill conversion and retention, A/B test reminder timing and messaging, and scale if more than 25% of subscribers keep reordering.

What I left out, and why

I plotted every initiative on impact against effort. Refill Club, bundles and reminders were quick wins; loyalty and the skin quiz were worth their higher effort. AI personalisation was deferred because simpler tools get most of the benefit within four months, and influencer campaigns because they're expensive, hard to predict, and don't help retention.
